# Ngulia in the future: research direction and protocol standardization

## Purpose of this note

Ngulia is both a long-term ringing programme and a natural experiment in how nocturnal migrants respond to light, mist, rain, topography and moonlight. Its greatest future value will come from preserving continuity with the historical series while recording the observation process well enough to separate:

1. how many birds are migrating overhead;
2. how weather, natural light and artificial light bring birds down;
3. how many grounded birds are available to catch;
4. how net configuration and team capacity determine the final catch.

This distinction is essential. The number ringed is not a direct count of the number migrating. It is the end product of migration intensity, attraction and grounding, bird behaviour, habitat, net effort, playback, staffing and processing capacity. The local radar study provides particularly strong evidence: substantial migration continued on clear, mist-free nights when birds were not caught ([Stark et al. 2019](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Stark_et_al___2019___Temporal_and_spatial_distribution_and_flight_directions_of_migratory_birds_in_Tsavo_West_National_P.pdf)).

The proposed strategy is therefore:

> Maintain a small, stable core protocol every year; attach question-specific modules that can change without compromising the long-term series.

## 1. Broad research directions

### 1.1 Long-term monitoring

Main questions:

- Is the timing, magnitude and species composition of passage changing?
- Are changes consistent among species, age classes and migration strategies?
- Are observed changes associated with rainfall, wind, mist, moonlight, habitat condition or dates of seasonal coverage?
- Can Ngulia provide an index of long-distance migrant populations after explicitly modelling effort and attraction conditions?

Ngulia has exceptional temporal depth and very large samples. The historical review already identified long-term compositional changes, including an increased proportion of Marsh Warblers and reductions in several other species ([Pearson et al. 2014](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/149917_394216_1_SM.pdf)). However, unequal dates, moon-centred coverage, changing lamps, net layouts, habitat and team sizes make absolute abundance trends difficult to infer.

The appropriate initial monitoring outputs are:

- standardized catch per unit effort, conditional on weather and light;
- seasonal phenology and its uncertainty;
- relative species composition;
- age ratios for species with reliable ageing criteria;
- mass, fat and moult distributions;
- an independent index of migration aloft whenever radar or another remote-sensing method is available.

Annual raw totals should still be reported, but they should not be presented alone as population indices.

### 1.2 Migration phenology and flight strategies

Main questions:

- When does passage start, peak and finish for each species and age class?
- How do wind, rainfall systems and the movement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone affect departure and passage?
- At what altitudes and in what directions do birds pass Tsavo?
- Does the date of a new moon determine what part of the migration season is sampled?

Historical coverage was concentrated around new moons, and the date of that window changes among years ([Pearson 2016](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/ajol_file_journals_523_articles_139731_submission_proof_139731_6157_372749_1_10_20160718.pdf)). This can create an apparent year effect when species differ in seasonal timing. The six-month radar deployment showed strong southward passage in boreal autumn, northward passage in March-April, and migration outside mist events ([Stark et al. 2019](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Stark_et_al___2019___Temporal_and_spatial_distribution_and_flight_directions_of_migratory_birds_in_Tsavo_West_National_P.pdf)).

The long-term programme should therefore distinguish the **annual operational window** from a smaller **fixed monitoring window** repeated on the same calendar dates whenever feasible. Even a modest continuous automated sensor deployed from October to January would reveal what occurs outside the staffed ringing session.

### 1.3 Attraction to light and the Ngulia grounding mechanism

Main questions:

- Which combination of migration intensity, cloud base, visibility, rain, moonlight and lamp output produces a fall?
- At what visibility or mist-height threshold do birds descend from overhead passage to the lights and then into vegetation?
- How far from the lodge does attraction begin?
- Do lamp spectrum, direction, intensity or intermittency alter attraction and disorientation?
- How rapidly do birds recover their direction after lights are reduced or extinguished?

This is arguably Ngulia's most distinctive and internationally important research avenue. Historical observations are unusually consistent:

- arrivals occur when low cloud or mist descends to bush-top height or below;
- showers increase grounding, and heavy rain can occasionally cause a fall without mist;
- attraction is reduced by moonlight;
- cloud remaining tens of metres above the lights may reveal migrants overhead without grounding them;
- lamp type, power and orientation have changed over the history of the project.

These observations appear throughout the early series and the synthesis by [Pearson & Backhurst (1976)](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Pearson_Backhurst___1976___The_Southward_Migration_of_Palaearctic_Birds_Over_Ngulia_Kenya.pdf) and [Pearson et al. (2014)](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/149917_394216_1_SM.pdf). In 2013-2015, for example, three additional halogen lamps totalling 3.5 kW were placed within 70 m of the north side of the lodge, facing north to northeast ([Pearson 2016](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/ajol_file_journals_523_articles_139731_submission_proof_139731_6157_372749_1_10_20160718.pdf)). Lamp history is therefore part of sampling effort, not merely background information.

Modern studies monitor attraction using combinations of weather or dedicated bird radar, thermal imaging, nocturnal flight-call recording and controlled light-on/light-off periods. At New York's Tribute in Light, radar and acoustic monitoring showed aggregation, slower and circular flight, and increased calling while lights were on; these effects disappeared when lights were extinguished ([Van Doren et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708574114)). Controlled experiments have also reconstructed three-dimensional flight paths near low-rise lighting ([Lao et al. 2021](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/potential-effect-low-rise-downcast-artificial-lights-nocturnally-migrating-land-birds)) and used thermal cameras to compare colour, intensity and continuous versus blinking light under different cloud conditions ([Rebke et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.02.029)).

Ngulia could make a unique contribution because it combines exceptionally strong light attraction with naturally varying mist, a large handled sample and a long historical record.

### 1.4 Moult, age, body condition and migration decisions

Main questions:

- Where and when do different species moult?
- Are moult strategies, age structure, wing length, mass or fat load changing?
- Do birds arriving under mist differ in condition from birds grounded by heavy rain?
- How do condition and moult state relate to onward migration?

This is already a proven strength. Ngulia material helped describe:

- partial autumn wing moult in River Warblers ([Pearson & Backhurst 1983](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Moult_in_the_river_Warbler_Locustella_fluviatilis.pdf));
- split wing-moult in Barred Warblers ([Lindström et al. 1993](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Ibis___October_1993___LINDSTROM___The_moult_of_Barred_Warblers_Sylvia_nisoria_in_Kenya_evidence_for_a_split_wingmoult.pdf));
- large interspecific differences in fuel loads and changes in mass through the night and day ([Pearson et al. 2014](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/149917_394216_1_SM.pdf));
- geographic differences in body-mass strategies across the East African flyway ([Yohannes et al. 2009](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/s10336_008_0357_z.pdf)).

This avenue does not require every bird to receive every measurement. A rigorously selected subsample can be more useful than irregular measurements from a larger number.

### 1.5 Migratory connectivity and origins

Main questions:

- Which breeding populations pass Ngulia?
- Where are their preceding stopovers and subsequent non-breeding areas?
- Do populations differ in timing, condition, moult or response to weather?
- Which stages of the annual cycle are most strongly associated with population change?

Ringing recoveries established connections spanning Eurasian breeding grounds, Middle Eastern passage sites and southern African non-breeding areas. Feathers collected at Ngulia have also identified probable stopover environments and geographic regions using stable isotopes ([Yohannes et al. 2005](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/Journal_of_Avian_Biology___2005___Yohannes___Stable_isotope_analyses_of_feathers_help_identify_autumn_stopover_sites_of.pdf); [Yohannes et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2007.0908-8857.03927.x); [Procházka et al. 2014](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/173484714X687073.pdf)).

Modern connectivity studies combine recoveries, recaptures, tracking, stable isotopes and genetics rather than treating them as competing methods. Recent integrated frameworks can join GPS or other tracking, live re-encounters, dead recoveries, capture-mark-recapture and population counts ([Gregory et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.14489)).

For Ngulia, the immediate opportunity is a well-governed **sample archive** rather than indiscriminate tagging:

- a defined set of target species;
- feather type and exact feather identity recorded;
- photographs, age, sex, moult, mass, wing and capture context linked to the sample;
- unique sample identifiers and freezer/dry-storage locations;
- permits, consent for future analyses and a sample-use policy;
- partnerships for isotopes, genomics, contaminants and disease work.

Light-level geolocators require recapture and are therefore best reserved for projects with a realistic recapture design at breeding or wintering sites. Automated radio telemetry such as Motus can track very small animals without recapture, but its value depends on a receiver network along the relevant route. A single Ngulia receiver would be useful for detecting already-tagged birds and for training, but a tagging project should begin only with collaborating stations elsewhere on the flyway ([Motus information for researchers](https://motus.org/get-involved/information-for-researchers/)).

### 1.6 Training, Kenyan leadership and an East African migration observatory

Main questions:

- Can Ngulia provide a structured pathway from extraction and recording to ringing, ageing, moult scoring and project leadership?
- Can annual training be documented as an output of the observatory?
- Can protocol standardization reduce dependence on a small number of experienced individuals?

Training is not secondary to research; it is part of the long-term viability of the programme. The Nairobi Ringing Group was created specifically to expand high-quality Kenyan ringing capacity ([Jackson 2000](https://doi.org/10.1080/00306525.2000.9639948)), and recent reports continue to identify Ngulia as an excellent training ground ([2024 session report](<../../data/02_reference/reports/2024 Ngulia Thanks_KWS.pdf>)).

Useful annual training outputs would include:

- number of participants by role and training level;
- supervised extractions, rings fitted and measurements taken;
- competency sign-offs using a stable checklist;
- short daily teaching sessions on identification, ageing, moult and data quality;
- Kenyan co-leadership of modules, datasets and publications;
- a post-season data audit involving trainees.

### 1.7 Afrotropical migration, disease and broader biodiversity

Ngulia also catches Afrotropical migrants, nightjars, quails and other taxa attracted during the same weather events. Historical work included blood parasites ([Peirce et al. 1977](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2028.1977.tb00380.x)), and the local literature reviews Afrotropical movements ([Turner & Backhurst 2020](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/ajol_file_journals_523_articles_198326_submission_proof_198326_6157_499015_1_10_20200805.pdf)).

This should be an optional module with a defined question, not an ever-expanding list of measurements. Disease sampling in particular needs explicit permits, biosafety, cold-chain and partner laboratory commitments before fieldwork.

#### Unresolved scope decision: Jacobin Cuckoo

The curated archive contains 99 Jacobin/Pied Cuckoo (*Clamator jacobinus*) records from ten seasons between 1993 and 2023. The identification is present in the source workbooks as `CLAJAC`, `OXYJAC`, Ngulia code `21`, and AFRING code `348`, so it is not an artefact introduced by the current taxonomy crosswalk.

The species is usually treated as an Afrotropical or Afro-Asian migrant, but some Asian populations undertake long-distance movements to East Africa. The existing ringing records do not establish whether the birds caught at Ngulia were African or originated in India or elsewhere in Asia. Consequently, biogeographic origin should not be inferred from the species name alone.

Retain these observations in the canonical ringing archive and GBIF export unless evidence shows that the source identification is wrong. Before finalizing a Palaearctic-focused website or analysis, decide explicitly whether eligibility means:

- belonging to a predefined Palaearctic species list;
- having a plausible Palaearctic or Asian migratory origin;
- or being any migrant captured at Ngulia.

Until that scope is agreed, flag Jacobin Cuckoo for review rather than deleting its records. Investigate origin using subspecies information, photographs or plumage notes, biometrics, moult and timing, recoveries, and future isotope or genetic samples where available.

## 2. The most promising programme

The strongest near-term programme is:

> **From migration aloft to birds in the net: quantify the Ngulia observation process.**

This programme would serve both fundamental research and long-term monitoring. It asks how overhead migration becomes light attraction, grounding and capture, and it creates the correction variables needed to interpret population trends.

It is promising because:

1. it addresses the main weakness of the historical dataset: incomplete effort and observation-process information;
2. it builds directly on the 2013-2014 radar result rather than duplicating it;
3. the light-mist interaction is globally distinctive;
4. it can start with affordable sensors and scale to radar or thermal tracking;
5. it improves every other analysis, including phenology, population trends, age ratios, body condition and species composition;
6. it creates clear roles for trainees in standardized observation, calibration and data management.

The core causal sequence to measure is:

**regional weather and migration pressure -> migration aloft -> illuminated mist/light exposure -> attraction and descent -> birds grounded in habitat -> birds encountering open nets -> extraction and processing capacity -> number ringed**

Each arrow can become a research question. It also makes clear why ERA5 and catch totals alone are insufficient.

## 3. Core protocol: collected every day, every year

### 3.1 Design principles

- Record events and intervals, not only a daily summary.
- Use timestamps in East Africa Time and store UTC as a derived field.
- Use one controlled site map and permanent IDs for every net, lamp, sensor and habitat sector.
- Keep raw observations unchanged; derive daily summaries later.
- Record zero effort explicitly. “No birds,” “nets closed” and “not observed” are different states.
- Separate night nets, dawn bush nets and swallow/playback nets in all data.
- Record changes when they happen; do not reconstruct them from memory at breakfast.
- Preserve a simple paper sheet as a field backup, but enter the same fields digitally.

### 3.2 Session and daily-operation record

For every operational date:

| Field | Minimum definition |
|---|---|
| `operation_date` | Date assigned using a documented night/day convention |
| `session_start`, `session_end` | First and last active observation timestamps |
| `observation_coverage` | Start/end of mist watch, including periods with no observer |
| `team_by_role` | Number of ringers, extractors, recorders, trainees and other helpers by time block |
| `planned_protocol` | Intended net set and planned operating window |
| `deviation_notes` | Brief reason for departures from the plan |
| `habitat_state` | Repeatable score plus fixed-point photographs |
| `safety_constraints` | Leopard, storm, guest disturbance, equipment failure or other reason affecting work |

Recent reports show why deviation reasons matter: rain, generator failures, wildlife, guest activity, quelea abundance and reduced team size have all delayed opening or forced closures ([2017 report](<../../data/02_reference/reports/2017 Ngulia RG Autumn 2017 Report.pdf>); [2023 report](<../../data/02_reference/reports/2023 Ngulia Report.pdf>); [2024 report](<../../data/02_reference/reports/2024 Ngulia Thanks_KWS.pdf>)).

### 3.3 Net deployment log

Do not record only “270 m of nets today.” Record an interval each time the state changes:

| Field | Definition |
|---|---|
| `net_id` | Permanent ID linked to coordinates, length, mesh, shelves and net line |
| `use_type` | Night, dawn bush, swallow/playback or special |
| `open_time`, `close_time` | Exact timestamps |
| `fraction_open` | 0-1 if partly furled or only part of a line is used |
| `check_interval_min` | Intended or observed round interval |
| `closure_reason` | Rain, wind, catch saturation, safety, playback end, wildlife, planned close, other |
| `playback_id` | None or permanent ID for sound and speaker configuration |

Derived metrics:

- **net-metre-hours** = sum of net length × hours open × fraction open;
- **percentage of planned net time open** = observed net-metre-hours / planned net-metre-hours;
- separate effort for night, dawn bush and swallow operations;
- time under each weather/light state;
- closures caused by saturation or capacity rather than environmental conditions.

Daily start time, end time, total net length and percentage open are useful summaries, but the interval log must be the source. Otherwise, a two-hour closure during the most intense fall is treated as equivalent to a closure during a quiet period.

### 3.4 Catch and processing log

Continue individual ringing records, linked to:

- exact capture time where feasible, otherwise net-round interval;
- `net_id`;
- capture method (net, hand inside lodge, collision/rescue);
- ring time;
- age, sex and ageing certainty;
- standardized biometrics;
- fat and muscle scores using one documented scale;
- moult fields using a species-appropriate standard;
- sample and photograph IDs;
- ringer, measurer and recorder IDs.

At least every 15 or 30 minutes during active catches, record:

- number of birds awaiting extraction, in bags and awaiting ringing;
- number of active extractors and ringing tables;
- whether nets were closed to control catch;
- any selective release or unprocessed species.

These variables identify saturation. Catch per net-metre-hour ceases to represent catchability when nets or teams are overwhelmed.

### 3.5 Habitat record

Habitat affects how long grounded birds remain and how they encounter dawn nets. Elephant-driven changes from dense bush to open grassland were already noted as a major alteration to catch conditions ([Pearson 2016](../../data/02_reference/publications/pdfs/ajol_file_journals_523_articles_139731_submission_proof_139731_6157_372749_1_10_20160718.pdf)).

Minimum annual record:

- fixed GPS photo points, with the same direction and focal length;
- vegetation cover within permanent sectors;
- mean and maximum vegetation height;
- proportion grass, open ground, shrub and tree cover;
- elephant damage and major changes to net lanes;
- a drone orthomosaic only if permitted and operationally realistic.

Fixed photographs and simple sector scores every year are more valuable than a complex habitat survey completed once.

## 4. Weather: what to measure and what equipment to buy

### 4.1 Use ERA5 for context, not for the local event

ERA5 remains valuable for:

- regional wind fields at multiple pressure levels;
- synoptic rainfall systems;
- temperature, pressure and broad cloud conditions;
- historical comparability back through the time series.

It should not be expected to reconstruct lodge-scale mist, drizzle or cloud touching the Ngulia ridge. These processes vary at spatial scales far below the ERA5 grid and are affected by local topography. The solution is to retain ERA5 and add continuous in-situ measurement.

### 4.2 Priority 1: visibility or mist

The most important new variable is **meteorological optical range (MOR)** measured by a forward-scatter visibility sensor near the light/net area. It directly quantifies the reduced visibility that observers currently describe as high mist, low mist, thick mist or clear.

Suitable instrument classes include:

- a research-grade visibility sensor, such as a Campbell Scientific CS120A;
- a present-weather and visibility detector, such as a Vaisala PWD-series unit, if precipitation classification is also required.

The [CS120A](https://www.campbellsci.com/products/cs120a) reports MOR using infrared forward scatter and is designed for continuous fog and mixed-weather measurement. Vaisala [PWD instruments](https://www.vaisala.com/en/products/instruments-sensors-and-other-measurement-devices/weather-stations-and-sensors/pwd22-52) combine MOR with, depending on model, precipitation type, intensity and accumulation.

Recommended logging:

- raw 1-minute values continuously from at least October through January;
- retain minimum, mean, maximum and variability for 5- and 10-minute summaries;
- preserve sensor status and contamination flags;
- service and calibrate according to the manufacturer;
- place the sensor where it samples air representative of the illuminated approach, without being directly saturated or heated by lamps.

MOR at one point will not describe the full three-dimensional cloud field, so it should be paired with a camera and human observations.

### 4.3 Priority 2: compact automatic weather station

Install a station recording at least:

- air temperature;
- relative humidity and dew point;
- barometric pressure;
- wind speed, gust and direction;
- precipitation onset, intensity and accumulation.

A compact all-in-one station such as a [Vaisala WXT536](https://docs.vaisala.com/r/M211607EN-AA/en-US/GUID-3D9CAFB5-35B5-4215-A5C6-37A17565D92E/GUID-E6912022-5F36-44DF-90CA-2721236D948C) measures the six main variables with few moving parts. Equivalent research-grade systems are also suitable. The purchase decision should consider local technical support, replaceable components, power demand, data access and calibration—not only headline accuracy.

For rainfall, a separate high-quality tipping-bucket or weighing gauge provides a useful reference because intense tropical rain and wind can challenge compact acoustic rain sensors. If distinguishing drizzle, rain and particle size becomes a core question, add a present-weather sensor or optical disdrometer in a later phase.

### 4.4 Priority 3: fixed camera and visual targets

Install a weatherproof camera in a fixed position looking across the illuminated airspace and vegetation. Use:

- fixed exposure, white balance, focal length and capture interval;
- synchronized timestamps;
- visible distance markers or targets at known ranges and heights;
- a wide image every minute and higher-rate recording during events;
- a second view of the sky/lamp beam if affordable.

Images can document:

- whether mist is below, within or above the lamp beam;
- horizontal visibility and spatial patchiness;
- rain streaks and sudden event transitions;
- lamp outages or intensity changes;
- approximate bird density and behaviour close to the lights.

A simple image-derived mist index can be calibrated against MOR. This is likely to reproduce the biologically relevant “illuminated mist” better than a reanalysis product or a single humidity threshold.

### 4.5 Manual weather observations remain necessary

At 15-minute intervals during mist watch, and whenever conditions change, record:

- no cloud / high cloud / cloud above lights / mist in lights / mist at bush-top / mist below bush-top;
- observer-estimated visibility category;
- no rain / drizzle / light / moderate / heavy rain;
- stars visible and moon visible;
- wind category if the automatic station is unavailable;
- birds not detected / heard only / passing through beam / circling / descending / grounded;
- observer ID.

The manual series provides continuity with historical language and training data for camera classification. Sensors should improve the human observations, not erase them.

### 4.6 Is a ceilometer needed?

A ceilometer measures cloud-base height and vertical visibility and would be scientifically useful, particularly for distinguishing cloud 30-100 m above the lights from cloud at ground level. However, it is substantially more expensive and demanding than a visibility sensor, and dense fog can complicate cloud-base retrieval.

Recommendation: do not make it the first purchase. First establish MOR + weather station + fixed camera. Borrow or rent a ceilometer for one pilot season, or add one only after the initial data show that cloud-base height cannot be recovered adequately from the camera, targets and MOR.

## 5. Light: what exactly should be recorded?

“Lights on” is not an adequate measurement. Record three layers.

### 5.1 Lamp configuration and operation

Create a permanent lamp inventory:

- `lamp_id`, technology and model;
- rated and measured electrical power;
- spectral description or correlated colour temperature;
- height, coordinates, azimuth, elevation and beam angle;
- distance to each net line;
- diffuser, reflector and shielding;
- installation and replacement dates.

Log every on/off, dimming, failure and generator interruption as a timestamped interval. An inexpensive electrical power logger or smart current meter on the lamp circuit is more reliable than a daily note.

### 5.2 Light reaching the environment

At permanent measurement points, record:

- horizontal and vertical illuminance in lux at net and vegetation height;
- zenith or all-sky brightness;
- calibrated photographs of the beam and sky;
- moon altitude, azimuth and illuminated fraction as derived variables;
- whether cloud obscures the moon.

Use the same meter, orientation and points each year. Measure a full spatial grid at installation and whenever lamps change; continuous logging is needed at only a few fixed reference points.

Lux captures human photopic illumination but not the full avian spectral response. Therefore, measure lamp spectra with a calibrated spectrometer once per season and after any bulb or fixture change. A continuous spectrometer is not initially necessary.

### 5.3 Light visible in mist

The biologically relevant stimulus may be the brightness and volume of illuminated droplets rather than lamp power alone. Derive image-based measures such as:

- mean radiance or pixel intensity within fixed beam regions;
- apparent beam area;
- contrast between beam and background sky;
- visibility of fixed targets;
- temporal flicker or generator instability.

These metrics should be calibrated against lamp state, lux, MOR and manual mist category.

## 6. Optional modern research modules

### Module A: passive acoustics

Deploy a weatherproof ultrasonic-capable or high-quality audible recorder directed upward throughout the season. Automated acoustic monitoring can estimate nocturnal calling activity and provide more species information than radar, although calling propensity differs among species and may itself increase under disorientation. A recent automated pipeline recovered nightly timing and activity for multiple migrant species at much lower cost than radar ([Van Doren et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.14342)).

At Ngulia, begin with an annotation project:

- build a verified library of flight calls from known or strongly supported species;
- retain raw audio;
- link recordings to radar, camera, weather, light and catch intervals;
- do not initially interpret call count as bird count.

### Module B: thermal or low-light imaging

A radiometric thermal camera can detect flight paths near lights without adding illumination. It can quantify:

- arrivals per unit time;
- flight height and direction close to the lodge;
- circling, descent and departure;
- responses during short light changes;
- birds present when no capture is attempted.

A single camera gives two-dimensional tracks; two synchronized cameras or one thermal camera plus radar can recover stronger three-dimensional inference. This is a strong medium-budget pilot before buying radar.

### Module C: dedicated bird radar

Radar is the best independent measure of migration aloft. Different systems answer different questions:

- vertical-looking or fixed-beam bird radar: migration traffic rate and altitude;
- scanning marine radar: local direction and relative intensity, but less reliable absolute intensity;
- automated tracking radar: individual three-dimensional tracks and response to lights.

Validation work shows that radar types agree well on seasonal patterns but differ in absolute intensity and strengths; species inference still requires complementary observations ([Nilsson et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.13174)).

Because Ngulia already hosted a successful radar deployment, the next radar project should be hypothesis-driven:

- repeat a comparable seasonal deployment to assess long-term change;
- combine radar with MOR, lamp output and thermal/acoustic sensors;
- measure clear-night passage as the denominator for attraction and capture;
- test behaviour during ethically approved light-on/light-off intervals.

### Module D: targeted biological subsampling

For selected common species, take a systematic sample—for example, the first eligible bird in each hour or a pre-declared number per species/day—for:

- full standardized biometrics;
- detailed moult score;
- standardized dorsal, wing and tail photographs with scale and colour card;
- one specified feather;
- blood or other material only under an approved project.

Systematic selection prevents interesting-looking birds or quiet periods from dominating the archive.

### Module E: tracking and connectivity

Potential tools:

- ring recoveries and foreign controls;
- stable isotopes;
- population genomics/genoscapes;
- light-level geolocators;
- automated VHF telemetry/Motus;
- very small GPS or multi-sensor tags where species size and retrieval permit.

Choose a tool only after specifying the spatial resolution, sample size, recovery probability and partner network required. At present, a sample archive plus coordinated flyway partnerships is likely to yield more durable value than purchasing many tags without receivers or a recapture plan.

## 7. A safe design for studying light attraction

Any manipulation of lights must prioritize bird welfare, lodge safety, KWS permissions and the integrity of the long-term operation. A suitable progression is:

### Phase 1: observation only

For one full season, measure existing variation in lamp state, MOR, rain, moon visibility, camera activity, acoustic activity, net effort and catch. Generator interruptions already create unplanned contrasts, but they should not be treated as randomized experiments.

### Phase 2: short paired contrasts

After ethical and operational approval, use randomized or balanced short blocks, for example:

- standard light versus reduced light;
- existing spectrum versus an alternative fixture;
- continuous versus intermittent light;
- fixed orientation A versus B.

Record radar or thermal response before, during and after each block. Catch should not be the only response because closing or changing lights changes the probability of birds reaching nets.

Predefine immediate shutdown criteria based on:

- dangerous aggregation or collision;
- heavy rain or unsafe extraction conditions;
- processing backlog;
- welfare observations.

Do not test many light properties simultaneously. A single well-replicated contrast across weather conditions is more interpretable than a complicated sequence of colours, intensities and directions.

### Phase 3: adaptive conservation protocol

The final application may be an evidence-based rule such as:

- when migration aloft is high and MOR crosses a threshold, operate a defined light configuration;
- reduce or extinguish lights when aggregation or collision risk exceeds a threshold;
- open only the net capacity that the team can service safely.

This could make Ngulia both a research observatory and a demonstration of responsible management of light-attracted migrants.

## 8. Equipment priorities

### Essential purchase package

Buy first:

1. forward-scatter visibility/MOR sensor;
2. compact research-grade automatic weather station;
3. independent rain gauge;
4. fixed weatherproof camera, known-distance targets and reliable time synchronization;
5. electrical logger for each lamp circuit;
6. calibrated lux meter or fixed illuminance logger;
7. datalogger, local storage, backup power and spare cables/connectors.

This package directly addresses the most important uncertainty and should operate beyond the staffed ringing dates.

### Enhanced package

Add:

1. present-weather detector or optical disdrometer;
2. upward-facing acoustic recorder;
3. thermal camera;
4. sky-quality or all-sky brightness sensor;
5. access to a calibrated spectrometer for seasonal lamp measurements;
6. second visibility or camera station if mist is spatially heterogeneous.

### Ambitious collaborative package

Consider:

1. dedicated bird radar;
2. ceilometer;
3. synchronized multi-camera thermal tracking;
4. Motus receiver station as part of a wider flyway network;
5. laboratory partnerships for isotopes, genomics, disease or contaminants.

Before procurement, obtain at least three complete quotes including datalogger, mast, enclosure, lightning protection, solar/battery capacity, shipping to Kenya, import costs, calibration, training, spares and local servicing. A cheaper sensor with inaccessible raw data or no regional support can become the more expensive option.

---

## 9. Implementation over three seasons

### Before season 1

- agree on the core research objectives and a data dictionary;
- map and permanently ID all nets, net lines, lamps, cameras and sensors;
- create paper and digital interval logs;
- install MOR, weather, rain, camera, lamp-state and light measurements;
- define the fixed monitoring date window;
- select two to four focal species for detailed moult/condition work;
- prepare training materials and competency records.

### Season 1: baseline and calibration

- make no planned light manipulation;
- run continuous sensors;
- compare manual mist categories with MOR and images;
- quantify missingness and clock drift daily;
- evaluate whether fixed camera views capture cloud height and spatial patchiness;
- test acoustic recording and annotation on a pilot basis;
- complete a post-season data audit before analysis.

Main output: a validated observation protocol and the first model of catchability.

### Season 2: replication and one focused experiment

- repeat the unchanged core protocol;
- add thermal imaging or borrowed radar;
- run one ethically approved, pre-registered light contrast;
- start the systematic sample archive;
- publish the field protocol and data dictionary.

Main output: a causal analysis of attraction under different visibility, rain and moon conditions.

### Season 3: monitoring model and expansion

- repeat the core protocol again;
- estimate migration aloft -> attraction -> grounding -> capture relationships;
- determine which sensors are essential for long-term operation;
- assess whether radar, ceilometer or a flyway telemetry partnership justifies sustained investment;
- produce standardized annual indicators with uncertainty.

Main output: a defensible long-term monitoring framework rather than raw annual totals alone.

## 10. Priority analyses

### 10.1 Catchability model

Response:

- birds caught per 10- or 30-minute interval, by species or species group.

Predictors:

- independent migration intensity from radar, where available;
- MOR and camera-derived illuminated-mist index;
- rain intensity and type;
- wind, temperature and pressure tendency;
- moon altitude, moon visibility and illumination;
- lamp configuration and measured illumination;
- net-metre-hours;
- habitat sector;
- playback;
- team capacity and saturation state;
- time of night and date within season.

The goal is not merely prediction. It is to estimate how strongly each stage of the observation process changes the catch.

### 10.2 Phenology and trend model

Estimate species-specific seasonal curves while accounting for:

- unequal annual date coverage;
- effort;
- weather and light attraction;
- protocol era;
- habitat change;
- observation-process uncertainty.

Report both:

- standardized Ngulia catch index;
- radar-based passage index when available.

Do not call either an absolute population estimate without external count or demographic information.

### 10.3 Condition and moult

For focal species:

- mass corrected for structural size, time of capture and date;
- fat and muscle distributions;
- moult strategy and feather-generation categories;
- age-specific timing;
- associations with rain versus mist grounding;
- repeatability among trained measurers.

### 10.4 Population dynamics

Ngulia alone cannot estimate every demographic rate. Its future role in population dynamics is to contribute:

- standardized passage indices;
- age ratios;
- condition and moult data;
- recoveries and re-encounters;
- migratory connectivity;
- exposure to environmental conditions during passage.

These can later enter integrated population models together with breeding counts, productivity and capture-recapture data from partner programmes. Integrated population models explicitly combine count and mark-recapture information while accounting for observation error ([Ahrestani et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.1493)).

## 11. Minimum daily checklist

Before operations:

- clocks synchronized;
- sensors running and storage checked;
- lamp configuration confirmed;
- planned nets and team roles recorded;
- fixed camera view checked.

During the night and morning:

- mist watch coverage logged;
- weather/light state every 15 minutes and at every change;
- every lamp change timestamped;
- every net opening, partial closure and reopening timestamped;
- staffing and backlog state recorded at regular intervals;
- safety and protocol deviations recorded immediately.

After operations:

- reconcile birds, rings and totals;
- close all net intervals;
- confirm sensor files and make a second copy;
- photograph habitat/event conditions if relevant;
- write a short factual daily narrative;
- list unresolved data issues while memories are fresh.

At the end of the season:

- freeze the raw data;
- document corrections separately;
- publish an effort/weather/light summary alongside the biological totals;
- archive calibration, maintenance and equipment-change records;
- hold a team review of what should remain unchanged next year.

## 12. Decisions that should be made collectively

1. What is Ngulia's primary long-term estimand: passage intensity, standardized catch, composition, phenology, demographic condition, or a defined combination?
2. What calendar dates form the fixed monitoring window?
3. Which existing net lines and lamp configuration define the reference protocol?
4. Which focal species receive detailed ageing, moult and sampling?
5. What level of catch triggers net closure for welfare and data-quality reasons?
6. Who owns each data stream and who completes daily quality control?
7. What are the policies for sample access, authorship, Kenyan leadership and external collaboration?
8. Which instruments can be maintained and calibrated locally for at least five years?

These decisions matter more than choosing a particular brand of sensor.

## Conclusion

Ngulia should not become a collection of unrelated add-on projects. Its future strength lies in a stable observatory design:

- **core:** standardized ringing, exact net effort, team capacity, habitat, local weather, mist and light;
- **mechanism:** quantify how migration aloft becomes attraction, grounding and capture;
- **modules:** moult/condition, acoustics, thermal imaging, radar, connectivity, genetics or disease;
- **capacity:** structured training and Kenyan scientific leadership;
- **interpretation:** standardized catch and passage indicators with explicit observation error.

The first investment should be in measuring the phenomenon Ngulia is famous for: real, local, illuminated mist. A visibility sensor, automatic weather station, fixed camera and complete lamp/net interval logs will improve the scientific value of every bird ringed and make future radar, tracking or population modelling much more powerful.
